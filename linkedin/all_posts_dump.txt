UnitedHealth Group just sold part of its Florida clinics, run under Optum's WellMed brand, to private equity firm TPG.

CFO Wayne DeVeydt said what every executive says here: they didn't need the cash, just a local partner to help the clinics grow. Fair, especially with Optum Health posting a $278 million loss last year.

But step back and look at the structure. One company can own the insurer, the doctors, and the clinics, and share that ownership with outside investors. Everyone in that chain wants more revenue, and revenue in healthcare comes from how claims get priced, coded, and paid.

If everyone touching a claim also profits from it, who's actually checking it for you?
Know who owns your providers. Know who checks your claims. Make sure they aren't the same people.

Returning from a brief hiatus to today’s #FDA public meeting on the #PDUFA VIII draft agreement. (Why Roman numerals like the Super Bowl? 🤔) 
  
When enacted in 1992, PDUFA was a bare 8 pages: collect fees to meet human drug review performance goals (specified in FDA letters printed in the Congressional Record). In 1992, we authorized $36 million in fees. The FY2026 revenue target is $1.43 billion.

After only five years, FDA credited PDUFA with reducing review times and an NDA backlog. By 2007, more than 1,000 employees were supported by user fees. Today, it’s more than 5,000 FDA staff in the human drug program.

   ► One perennial, albeit questionable, criticism of the program is that making FDA reliant on industry funding is a recipe for agency capture. Back in the 1950s, political scientist Marver Berstein defined agency “decay” or “capture”  as “a subtle relationship in which the mores, attitudes, and thinking of those regulated come to prevail in the approach and thinking of [the regulator].”

   ► Questionable because of a lack of substantive evidence. Yet, HHS Secretary RFK Jr said in November 2024 that President-elect Trump directed him to reduce “corruption and conflicts” at FDA. In April 2025, RFK Jr accused FDA staff to their faces of being “sock puppets” for regulated industries- part of the Agency’s “aggressive suppression”, as he posted in October 2024, of “psychedelics, peptides, stem cells, raw milk, hyperbaric therapies, chelating compounds, ivermectin, hydroxychloroquine, vitamins, clean foods, sunshine, exercise, nutraceuticals”.

   ► One of Kennedy’s solutions was to “reform” PDUFA, which meant “reduce”. But PDUFA VIII doesn’t do that, because RFK Jr can’t find $1.4 billion in annual replacement funding.

Yet “reform” for “radical transparency” is possible. In 2007, Congress mandated public meetings with non-industry stakeholders “prior to beginning negotiations”, “hold discussions with representatives of patient and consumer advocacy groups … once every month during negotiations”, and hold a public meeting and docket for comments on the draft agreement, “revis[ing] such recommendations as necessary.” (FFDCA section 736B(f))

What these mandates lack is accountability. FDA receives public pre-negotiation priorities to boost #innovation and meet #publichealth needs, but isn’t obliged to bring them to the table. “Discussions” may only be reportage that doesn’t alter negotiations. And nothing compels FDA to revise the draft agreement or account for what is or is not included from the public priorities the Agency received at the onset.

In the face of the intense skepticism voiced by the HHS Secretary, Congress may consider putting teeth into the law- requiring FDA to more directly represent “patient and consumer advocacy” priorities during industry negotiations and spelling out -in fora like today’s public meeting- how a PDUFA draft agreement reflects the public input it received at the onset.

Can't let the day go by without reflecting on today's Michigan Health & Hospital Association Small & Rural Hospital Legislative Day. 

It was a privilege - always is and will be - to join hospital leaders and staff as they shared the impact their hospitals have on their communities and the work it takes to keep care close to home.

What struck me most were the personal connections shared by legislators and their staff:"My brother-in-law was just treated there after a farming accident." "My mom worked there."

Those stories are a reminder that small and rural hospitals are anchor institutions woven into the fabric of our communities, providing care and stability. These are the dots we need to connect. 

Policies that reduce hospital resources without addressing the true drivers of healthcare costs are not solutions. They simply strain an already fragile system and put local access to care at risk. 

Rural healthcare is healthcare. Healthcare is health. And that is something  we all should care about.
